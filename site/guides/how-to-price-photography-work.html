How to price photography work

Photographers tend to price from what other photographers charge, then discover that the hours behind each booking are far longer than the shoot itself. Editing, travel, client calls, gear and the days nobody books all have to be paid for by the shoots that do happen. This guide builds your prices from the income you need, through day rates and packages, to prints and licensing. Each step links to the calculator that does the arithmetic.

1. Work out how many days you can really sell

Start by counting bookable shoot days, not working days. A week of work for a photographer usually holds a few shoots and a lot of editing, admin, marketing and gear maintenance. Some work is seasonal, so a busy summer has to carry a quiet winter. Look back at last year's calendar if you have one and count the days you actually shot. That number, not an ideal week, is what your rate has to be built on. The photographer day rate calculator asks for it directly, because it changes the answer more than any other input.

2. Turn income and costs into a day rate

Add the income you want for the year to your business costs: camera bodies and lenses spread over their useful life, insurance, software subscriptions, storage and backup, your website, travel and accounting. Divide by the bookable shoot days and you have the day rate your business needs. The photographer day rate calculator does this and also gives a half-day rate and the real hourly figure once editing and admin time are counted. If you also take hourly work, the general hourly rate calculator is a useful cross-check. A half-day is rarely worth exactly half a day, because the travel and setup are the same and the rest of the day is often hard to sell.

3. Build packages from hours, not from round numbers

A package is a promise of a certain number of hours and deliverables, so price it from those hours. Count the shoot time, the editing time, the travel time and the client meetings, multiply by your rate, add travel costs and any products included, such as an album or a set of prints, at their marked-up price. The photography package price calculator puts all of this together into a package price. Build two or three packages this way and check that each one earns your rate. Clients like a choice, and a clear difference in what each package includes makes the choice easy.

4. Know what each edited image costs you

Clients often ask for more images, or for a few extra edits after delivery. To answer, you need to know what one delivered image costs you. Spread the shoot and editing time across the number of images you deliver and you have a cost per image. The cost per edited image calculator does this and suggests a price for extra images. It is also a useful check on your packages: if a package promises a large number of fully edited images, the editing time can quietly double the hours you planned. Promise a range or a minimum rather than an exact count.

5. Mark up prints and products properly

Prints, albums and wall art are products, and products need a margin as well as a cost. Start from the lab price, add shipping and packaging, and then price for the time you spend on ordering, checking proofs and handling reprints. Decide whether you work from a markup on cost or a target margin on the selling price; the same percentage gives a different price depending on which you use. The print markup calculator shows the print price either way. If you sell prints online through a fulfilment service, the print on demand profit calculator shows what is left after the platform takes its share.

6. Price licensing separately from the shoot

For commercial work, the shoot fee pays for your time and the licence pays for the use of the images. A business that uses a photo in a national advertising campaign for several years gets far more value than one that uses it on a single social post, and the price should reflect that. Start from a base licence fee and scale it by duration, territory, media and exclusivity. The licensing fee calculator applies those factors so the fee is consistent from one client to the next. Write the usage terms on the quote and the invoice, so an extension of use becomes a new fee rather than a favour.

7. Take a deposit and set the payment terms

A booked date blocks out work you could have taken, so ask for a retainer or deposit when the client books. A non-refundable booking fee with the balance due before the shoot or before delivery is common for weddings and events; commercial clients often expect invoices on terms. The deposit split calculator divides a fee into a deposit and stage payments. For invoiced work, the invoice due date calculator sets the due date for terms like net 30, and the late fee calculator shows what a late fee adds. Holding back final delivery until the balance is paid is the simplest protection you have. The mistakes that cost the most

  • Pricing from the shoot hours alone. Editing, travel and client communication often take longer than the shoot. Price the whole job.
  • Selling prints at lab cost plus a little. Ordering, checking and reprints are your time. A thin markup turns products into unpaid work.
  • Giving unlimited usage by default. Without written licence terms, a commercial client can use the images anywhere, forever, for the price of the shoot.
  • Delivering before the final payment. Once the gallery is delivered, the incentive to pay the balance drops. Make final payment a condition of delivery.
