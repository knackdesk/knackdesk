Why change orders need their own price

Extra work requested after the contract is signed is small, unplanned and disruptive: it interrupts the sequence, needs a separate materials run and often a return visit. Pricing it at cost, or waving it through as goodwill, gives away the margin on work that costs more per unit than the original job. The rule that keeps contracts healthy is to price every change at the same markup as the original quote and to put it in writing before the work starts, showing three figures: the original price, this change, and the new total. Those three figures are exactly what the calculator produces.

Own work and subcontractors

Many contractors apply a lower markup to subcontracted work, because the subcontractor carries their own overhead and the main contractor only coordinates. A separate markup field keeps that distinction. Use your real labour cost per hour, including employer contributions, so the markup is genuine profit and overhead rather than wage recovery. If you have not settled a standard markup, the contractor markup calculator derives one from your overhead and profit targets.

Watching the cumulative figure

The last line shows all change orders together as a share of the original contract. When it climbs past ten or fifteen percent, the project has drifted from its scope and the schedule and payment stages usually need revisiting too. The deposit split calculator re-stages payments against the revised total, and the job quote calculator builds the original price the changes are compared with.

Frequently asked questions

What about work removed from the scope?

Enter the removed work's cost as a negative change by agreement; a credit is usually at cost plus a smaller markup, and the arithmetic is the same with negative inputs handled by hand.

Should the change order include a schedule change?

Yes, in the document. The calculator covers price only.

Is my data stored?

No. Everything runs in your browser.
