What a retainer is buying

A retainer is a client paying for availability, not just hours. They get priority and a predictable bill; you get predictable income and less time selling. That trade justifies a discount on your hourly rate, but the discount should be modest, typically 5% to 15%, because the reserved hours are hours you cannot sell to anyone else. A retainer priced at a steep discount with no hour cap is the fastest way to turn a good client into an underpaid full-time job.

The three terms that make retainers work

A defined hour block. "Up to 20 hours a month" with a stated rate for hours beyond it. A rollover rule. Either unused hours expire monthly (simplest and most common) or roll over for one month only. Unlimited rollover builds a liability you will eventually have to deliver for free. A minimum term with notice. Three to six months with 30 days' notice protects both sides and is the real reason the discount exists. Put all three in the agreement and repeat the hour count on every invoice.

Reading the numbers

The effective hourly rate is the figure to compare with your target rate. If it is below your floor, reduce the discount or the hours rather than accept it. The "discount given over the term" line shows what the commitment is costing you in total; if that number makes you wince, the discount is too high. Frequently asked questions

Should the retainer be paid in advance?

Yes, at the start of each month. That is standard and is part of what the discount pays for.

What if the client regularly uses fewer hours?

Keep the fee; they are paying for availability. If it becomes a pattern, offer a smaller block at a slightly higher effective rate at the next renewal.

What about work outside the retainer scope?

Quote it separately as a project. Retainer hours should cover the ongoing, recurring work the agreement describes.
