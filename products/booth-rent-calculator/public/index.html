Booth Rent Calculator

Enter the weekly rent for your chair, booth, room or station, how many clients you see in a week, what the average client pays, the share of that spent on product and how many weeks a year you work. You get your weekly revenue, rent as a percentage of it, what you keep each week and each year, the rent you pay in a year and how many clients a week cover the rent on their own. It is written for independent stylists, barbers, nail and beauty technicians, massage therapists, personal trainers renting gym space and anyone else who rents a place to see clients by appointment. Nothing is sent anywhere.

In one sentence: Booth rent is a fixed weekly payment to a salon or studio for the use of a chair or room, which you keep paying whether your book is full or empty.

Formula: weekly revenue = clients per week × average ticket; rent % of revenue = weekly rent ÷ weekly revenue × 100; net per week = weekly revenue − weekly rent − weekly revenue × product cost % ÷ 100; net per year = net per week × weeks worked; annual rent = weekly rent × weeks worked; break-even clients = weekly rent ÷ (average ticket × (1 − product cost % ÷ 100)), rounded up.

What each input means

Weekly rent is the amount in your rental agreement. If you pay monthly, multiply the monthly figure by 12 and divide by 52 to get a weekly one. If the agreement adds charges for towels, laundry, card processing or a share of utilities, add those in too, because they are part of what the space costs you each week.

Clients per week is the number of people you actually saw, not the number you hoped to book. Your booking app or appointment book will give it to you; take the total for the last few months and divide by the number of weeks you worked. A single busy week makes a poor guide.

Average ticket is what one client pays for the service, before tips. Your card reader or booking system can usually show total takings for a period; divide by the number of clients in the same period. If you sell retail products, leave that income out, or include it only if you also include the cost of buying the stock.

Product cost is the share of your service revenue that goes on colour, treatments, disposables and anything else used up on clients. Add up your supplier invoices for a month and divide by service revenue for that month, then multiply by 100. As a renter you normally buy this yourself, which is one of the main differences from working on commission.

Weeks worked is the number of weeks a year you pay rent and see clients. If your agreement charges rent during your holidays, the cost does not stop when you do; in that case use 52 for rent and think about how much lower your client weeks really are.

Reading the result

With the example figures, 25 clients at 65 each bring in 1,625 a week. Rent of 300 is 18.46% of that. Taking off the rent and 10% for product leaves 1,162.50 a week, which over 50 working weeks is 58,125 a year, while the rent itself comes to 15,000. After product, each client leaves you 58.50, so 6 clients a week are enough to pay the rent; every client after the sixth is income for you.

Net here means before tax, insurance, education, tools and anything else you pay for outside the agreement. Treat it as what the chair earns, not what lands in your personal account.

The break-even client count is the figure to watch in a slow month. When you know that six clients cover the rent, a quiet week stops being vague worry and becomes a clear target. If the calculator says break-even is not possible, your ticket is zero or product takes the whole of it.

This page does not say what booth rent should cost or what share of revenue is reasonable. Rents depend on the location, the salon and what is included, and the only fair comparison is with your own takings. If the rent share rises over time, look at whether the rent went up, your client numbers fell or your prices have not moved.

Using it to plan

Before signing a new agreement, run the calculator with the rent on offer and the clients you are confident of bringing with you, not the ones you hope to win. Frequently asked questions

Should tips go into the average ticket?

No. Tips vary and are not something you can price on. Leaving them out gives a more cautious result.

Can I use it for a room in a spa or a slot in a gym?

Yes. Any fixed weekly payment for space you use to see clients works the same way.

Is my data stored?

No. Everything runs in your browser.
